Consider the Work of God

Wisdom is a defence on a good day. But what do you do on a crooked one? Solomon's answer isn't to fix it — it's to consider it.

Word and Faith Network · Ecclesiastes 7:11–14
"Wisdom is good with an inheritance: and by it there is profit to them that see the sun. For wisdom is a defence, and money is a defence: but the excellency of knowledge is, that wisdom giveth life to them that have it. Consider the work of God: for who can make that straight, which he hath made crooked? In the day of prosperity be joyful, but in the day of adversity consider: God also hath set the one over against the other, to the end that man should find nothing after him."
Ecclesiastes 7:11–14

Solomon spent a great deal of Ecclesiastes being honest about what money, pleasure, and achievement cannot do. So when he finally says something good about money — "money is a defence" — it's worth pausing on. He isn't contradicting himself. He's making a comparison, and comparisons only work when both sides are real. Money really does defend you from certain things: hunger, exposure, some kinds of trouble. Solomon doesn't deny that. He simply says wisdom defends you too, and then tells you exactly where the two defences stop being equal.

"The excellency of knowledge is, that wisdom giveth life to them that have it." Money can shelter you from a storm. It cannot give you life in the storm. That's the whole weight of this passage in one line — and it's the lens for everything that follows.

A Defence That Gives Life

Think about what a defence is actually for. A defence doesn't change the weather; it just determines what the weather can do to you. Money, insurance, savings, connections — these are all defences in that sense. They don't stop the crooked day from coming. They just soften what it costs you when it does. That's real, and Solomon doesn't sneer at it.

But wisdom does something money structurally cannot. It doesn't just soften the blow — it gives you life inside the blow. A well-funded person without wisdom can still fall apart in adversity, because their defence only ever covered their circumstances, never their soul. A wise person with far less in reserve can walk through the same adversity still standing, still clear-headed, still able to hear God — because their defence was never circumstantial to begin with.

"If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him."James 1:5, KJV

This is why Solomon can commend both in the same breath and still rank them. Keep your inheritance. Keep your provision. Just don't mistake either one for the thing that actually keeps you alive when the ground shifts.

Consider the Work of God

Verse thirteen is where this teaching turns from comfortable to confrontational: "Consider the work of God: for who can make that straight, which he hath made crooked?" Some things in your life are not bent because you failed. They are bent because God, for His own purposes, made them that way for a season — and no amount of striving, complaining, or clever strategy will straighten what He has not yet chosen to straighten.

Notice the instruction isn't "fix the work of God." It isn't even "understand the work of God." It's consider it. Look at it honestly. Stop pretending it isn't crooked, and stop assuming that because it's crooked, it's unsupervised. Those are two different errors, and most of us are tempted by one or the other — either denial that anything is wrong, or despair that God has stepped away from it.

"Woe unto him that striveth with his Maker! Let the potsherd strive with the potsherds of the earth."Isaiah 45:9, KJV

There is a kind of peace that only becomes available once you stop fighting the shape of a season you did not choose and could not have prevented. Not resignation — consideration. A sober, open-eyed look at what God is doing, trusting that the Hand that bent it is still the Hand that can, in His own time, make it straight.

The Day of Prosperity, The Day of Adversity

Verse fourteen gives the two postures Solomon expects a wise person to actually hold: joy in prosperity, and consideration in adversity. Most of us are only practiced at one. Some believers know exactly how to celebrate a good day but go completely silent before God in a hard one, as if prayer were only for emergencies and gratitude the only acceptable language in church. Others have made peace with hardship as a permanent identity, and have forgotten how to simply rejoice without guilt when God blesses them.

Solomon asks for both, deliberately, because both seasons are real and both are assigned. Joy isn't naive when the day is good. Consideration isn't unbelief when the day is hard. They are the two correct responses to two real conditions — and a mature faith knows which one the day in front of it is actually calling for.

Both From His Hand

The last line of this passage is the one that reframes everything before it: "God also hath set the one over against the other, to the end that man should find nothing after him." Prosperity and adversity are not accidents that happen to intersect with God's plan. Solomon says plainly that God Himself set them against one another — on purpose, so that you would never be able to audit Him. You cannot say, "God was only present in the good season," because adversity was His too. You cannot say, "God abandoned me in the hard season," because the prosperity before it, or the prosperity still to come, is His as well.

Both seasons carry His fingerprints precisely so you keep looking to Him in both — never self-sufficient in the good day, never without hope in the hard one. That is the purpose behind the pairing: not to confuse you, but to keep you leaning on the only One who holds both days in the same hand.

The Word for Today

Whatever today has handed you, consider the work of God in it honestly before you try to fix it, explain it, or run from it. If this is a day of prosperity, be joyful — fully, without apology. If this is a day of adversity, consider — don't panic, and don't pretend. Either way, remember that wisdom's excellency isn't that it exempts you from crooked seasons. It's that it gives you life inside them, until the Hand that bent the path is ready to make it straight.
