His Mercies Are New Every Morning

The same prophet who wept over the ruins of Zion also wrote, "Great is thy faithfulness." A study on how hope survives the worst chapter of your life.

Word and Faith Network · Lamentations 2 & 3
"This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope. It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness."
Lamentations 3:21–23

Lamentations is not a comfortable book. It was written by a man watching the city he loved reduced to rubble, and it does not rush past the grief to get to a tidy ending. Chapter 2 opens with some of the darkest language in all of Scripture: "How hath the Lord covered the daughter of Zion with a cloud in his anger! The Lord hath swallowed up all the habitations of Jacob, and hath not pitied." Jeremiah does not soften what he saw. He names it — judgment, ruin, a people brought low — because pretending the pain isn't real has never been the path to healing. It's the first step toward denial, not faith.

And yet this same book, only one chapter later, contains one of the most quoted verses of hope in the entire Bible: "His compassions fail not. They are new every morning." The question worth sitting with is not why the Bible contains both. It's how a man standing in chapter 2 ever gets to chapter 3 at all.

The Honesty of Lamentations 2

Before there is hope, there is honesty. Jeremiah doesn't talk himself out of his sorrow or spiritualize it away. "He hath thrown down in his wrath the strong holds of the daughter of Judah... he hath polluted the kingdom and the princes thereof." This is a man telling the truth about what collapse actually feels like — and Scripture lets him say it, in full, without interrupting him with a premature word of encouragement.

There is something important here for anyone walking through genuine loss. Grief that is named is grief that can move. Grief that is rushed past, minimized, or spiritually bypassed tends to resurface later, heavier than before. Jeremiah gives us permission to call the ruin what it is — not because God delights in despair, but because an honest lament is still, itself, an act of faith. He is still talking to God. He has not walked away.

"This I Recall to My Mind" — the Hinge of the Whole Book

Chapter 3 continues the lament for twenty verses — more affliction, more weeping, more of the same weight from chapter 2. Then, in verse 21, something shifts, and it is worth noticing exactly what causes it. Jeremiah does not say his circumstances changed. The city is still in ruins. He says: "This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope." Hope did not arrive because the situation got better. Hope arrived because he deliberately remembered something true about God in the middle of a situation that had not gotten better at all.

"I remember the days of old; I meditate on all thy works."Psalm 143:5, KJV

This is the pattern across Scripture: hope is rarely a feeling that shows up on its own. It is a decision to recall, in the present tense, who God has already proven Himself to be. Jeremiah's hope was not naive optimism that things would simply work out. It was a trained memory — reaching back for the character of God when the evidence in front of him said otherwise.

Mercies That Are New Every Morning

And then comes the verse the whole book is remembered for: "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." Notice what Jeremiah is not saying. He is not saying the ruin never happened, or that it didn't hurt. He is saying that in the middle of the ruin, God's mercy kept showing up — fresh, undiminished, on schedule, every single morning, whether Jeremiah felt strong enough to notice it or not.

"The LORD is good unto them that wait for him, to the soul that seeketh him."Lamentations 3:25, KJV

This is the promise underneath every hard season: God's mercy is not a one-time rescue that you either caught or missed. It renews. Whatever yesterday cost you, whatever you said or didn't say, whatever you got wrong — this morning carries a fresh supply of the same faithfulness that held Jeremiah together while he stood in the ashes of everything he loved. You are not required to have enough faith to last a lifetime. You only need enough to receive what's new today.

The Word for Today

If you are somewhere in your own Lamentations 2 right now — honest about the loss, not pretending it doesn't hurt — you are not disqualified from Lamentations 3. You are exactly where the hinge verse was written for. Recall to your mind what God has already proven about Himself. His compassions have not failed, not once, not toward you. They are new again this morning. Great is His faithfulness.
