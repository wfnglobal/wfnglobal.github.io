Nobody Lives to Himself

You didn't just get saved from something. You got bought by Someone. Your life, your death, your Monday morning — none of it is yours to run anymore.

Word and Faith Network · Romans 14:7-9
"For none of us liveth to himself, and no man dieth to himself. For whether we live, we live unto the Lord; and whether we die, we die unto the Lord: whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord's. For to this end Christ both died, and rose, and revived, that he might be Lord both of the dead and living."
Romans 14:7-9, KJV

Somebody reading this has been living like their life is a private project — their time, their plans, their body, their "me time," their decisions to make and unmake as they please. And Paul walks into that illusion with one sentence that ends it: none of us liveth to himself. Not the strong believer. Not the weak one. Not the one who fasts on Mondays or the one who doesn't. Nobody. The verse doesn't say you shouldn't live for yourself. It says you can't. The ownership was settled a long time before you woke up this morning.

Paul Wasn't Talking About Rules. He Was Talking About Ownership.

Read Romans 14 from the top and you'll find Paul in the middle of a church argument — one group judging another over food and holy days, each side certain the other was getting it wrong. And right when you expect Paul to settle the rule, he reaches past it for something bigger: it was never really about the calendar or the menu. It was about Whose house you think you're living in. "Who art thou that judgest another man's servant?" he asks a few verses earlier. Servant. That's the word that unlocks the whole chapter. You cannot set the rules in a house you don't own, and you cannot judge another man's servant when you're not the master either.

That's the quiet correction underneath every opinion you've ever held too tightly about somebody else's walk with God: you were never hired to run their life. You were bought to surrender yours.

None of Us Liveth to Himself — Not Even the Strong Ones

There's a version of independence that sounds like maturity but is actually just old rebellion with better manners. "I answer to God, not to people" is true — and it is often used to cover the deeper refusal: "I answer to no one, including God, about how I actually spend my days." Paul doesn't let that slide. He says none of us — not the disciplined, not the gifted, not the one who's been saved the longest — gets to live to himself. The cross didn't just forgive your sin. It transferred your title.

"Know ye not that ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God's."1 Corinthians 6:19-20, KJV

A bought thing doesn't get to vote on how it's used. That should feel like a loss for about four seconds — and then it should feel like the safest sentence you've read all week, because the One who bought you is not a tyrant counting your mistakes. He's the Lord who died to make the purchase in the first place.

Whether We Live, We Live Unto the Lord

Here is where the verse stops being a boundary and becomes an assignment. Paul doesn't just say you belong to the Lord in some passive, filed-away sense. He says your living is unto Him — aimed at Him, offered to Him, done for His sake whether anybody else notices or not. That changes what a Tuesday is. The meeting nobody will remember, the meal you cooked for a tired family, the patience you held onto when you had every right to snap — none of it was private. All of it was unto the Lord, whether you dedicated it out loud or not.

That should relieve some of you and convict the rest of you, often in the same sentence. Relieve you, because the small faithful things you do where no one is watching are not wasted — they're received. Convict you, because the things you do carelessly, assuming nobody's keeping count, were never off the record either. Living unto the Lord covers the whole territory, not just the parts you've decided to spiritualize.

And Whether We Die, We Die Unto the Lord

Paul doesn't stop at living. He follows the ownership all the way to the grave, because that's the only place some of us still think we get the final say. We'll surrender our Tuesdays, but we want to negotiate the ending — when, how, on what terms. And Paul says even that belongs to Him. Not because death is something to chase or treat lightly, but because a life that's been His all along doesn't suddenly become yours again at the finish line. The same hand that held your first breath holds your last one too.

There is a strange peace available only to people who've actually believed this — not people who merely quote it. If your life is the Lord's and your death is the Lord's, then there is no moment on the calendar where you're suddenly on your own, managing an emergency God stepped back from. You are never off the clock He's keeping.

We Are the Lord's — Settled, Not Debated

"Whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord's." Read that sentence slowly. It is not a hope. It is not a goal you're working toward. It is a present-tense fact, stated as settled as your own name. Whatever season you're in right now — thriving, struggling, waiting, grieving, celebrating — the ownership underneath all of it has not changed and is not up for renegotiation. You don't earn your way back into belonging to Him when you've had a good week, and you don't get quietly repossessed when you've had a bad one. You are the Lord's. Full stop.

"I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."Romans 8:38-39, KJV

That's not a different author being more generous than Paul was in chapter 14. That's the same conviction, stated twice, because some truths need to be said more than once before we actually believe them.

Why Christ Did It: To Be Lord of Both

Paul closes the thought by telling you exactly why the cross happened the way it did: "For to this end Christ both died, and rose, and revived, that he might be Lord both of the dead and living." Not Lord of your Sunday only. Not Lord of the parts of your story you're proud of. Lord of the dead and the living — meaning there is no room in your life, your history, or your future where His authority runs out. He didn't die to collect admirers. He died, rose, and lives again to hold a title that covers every square inch of your existence, including the parts you haven't surrendered yet.

That is either terrifying or it is the best news you'll hear this week, and it depends entirely on who you think is holding the title. A stranger with that kind of authority over you would be unbearable. A Savior who already proved what He'll do with it — bleed for you, rise for you, intercede for you — makes that same authority the safest address you could ever live at.

The Word for Today

Somebody has been quietly running their own life again — your time, your grudges, your opinions about somebody else's walk with God, even your plans for how your story ends — and calling it independence instead of what it actually is: forgetting Whose you are. Paul's answer isn't a new set of rules. It's a correction of ownership. None of us lives to himself. Whether you live, live unto the Lord. Whether you die, die unto the Lord. Either way, you are His — not someday, not once you clean up, but right now, as settled as it will ever get. Christ did not die and rise to be admired from a distance. He did it to be Lord of you, completely — the parts you've surrendered, and the parts you haven't gotten around to yet.
