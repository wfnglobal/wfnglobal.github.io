Thoughts of Peace, Not Evil

Judah heard seventy years before they heard home — and God still called the plan peace. If He can call that peace, He can call whatever you're walking through right now by its true name too.

Word and Faith Network · Jeremiah 29:11
"For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end."
Jeremiah 29:11, KJV

Jeremiah 29:11 gets quoted on graduation cards and framed above desks so often that it's easy to miss what it actually cost to say. This was not a promise whispered to people in comfort. It was mailed into exile — to a nation sitting in Babylon, far from home, watching false prophets promise a quick return that God had already told them wasn't coming. Into that exact room, God sends a letter that opens with seventy years and closes with a plan called peace. If you only remember the ending, you miss the part that makes it believable: God named the wait before He named the welfare, and called both of them good.

A Promise Delivered Into Exile, Not Instead Of It

Read the verses just before this one and the comfort gets harder, and more honest. "For thus saith the LORD, That after seventy years be accomplished at Babylon I will visit you, and perform my good word toward you" (Jeremiah 29:10). God doesn't promise to skip the exile. He promises to be found inside it, and to be faithful on the other side of it. That's a very different message than the one the false prophets were selling — the ones telling Judah the captivity would be short, the crisis would pass quickly, nothing would really have to change. God corrected that lie first, with a number that must have landed like a weight: seventy years. Then, and only then, did He hand them the word peace.

Somebody reading this has been hoping for the short version of their story — the quick turnaround, the fast exit, the testimony with no hard middle. God's word to Judah says the comfort was never in avoiding the valley. It was in who was waiting for them on both sides of it.

Seventy Years Is Not a Mistake — It's a Plan With a Date

Seventy years is specific. Not "a while," not "eventually," not "when you've learned your lesson" — a number, attached to a promise, kept by a God who does not forget what He said even across seven decades of silence from His side of the conversation. That's the detail that should steady you more than the word peace itself: this wasn't an improvised comfort offered after the plan fell apart. The exile and the end of the exile were the same plan, spoken by the same God, on the same day.

Whatever delay you've been calling evidence that God forgot you, Judah's seventy years says otherwise. A long wait is not proof of an absent God. Sometimes it's proof of a God specific enough to put a number on His own faithfulness before you ever asked Him to.

God Calls It Peace Before You Can See Why

"Thoughts of peace, and not of evil." Say that slowly over the version of your life that doesn't look peaceful yet. God is not describing the outcome after the fact, the way we do when a hard season finally resolves and we can see the lesson in hindsight. He's naming the plan while Judah is still in Babylon, still homesick, still decades from the ending. That's either reckless optimism or it's the voice of Someone who can see the whole timeline at once — and Scripture never once calls God reckless.

"For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts."Isaiah 55:8-9, KJV

That's why your read of your own situation and God's read of it can disagree without either of you being dishonest. You're reading it from inside year three of seventy. He's reading it from the end, where the word He already spoke over it has had time to become visible.

An Expected End Is Not a Guess

The Hebrew behind "expected end" carries the sense of a future and a hope — not a maybe, not a possibility among several, but a destination God has already set and is walking you toward. That matters because most of us treat our future like weather: unpredictable, maybe good, maybe not, best not to get your hopes up. Jeremiah 29:11 doesn't let you file your future under weather. It tells you Someone wrote the forecast before the storm started, and the forecast says peace.

This doesn't mean nothing hard will happen between now and then — Judah still had seventy years of exile to walk through, the same seventy years God had already named. It means the hard years are not the whole story, and they're not the ending. They're the middle of a plan whose last page was settled before the first page was written.

What to Do With a Promise That Hasn't Arrived Yet

Three verses later, God tells Judah exactly how to live inside the wait: "Then shall ye call upon me, and ye shall go and pray unto me, and I will hearken unto you. And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:12-13). The promise of peace was never meant to make you passive. It was meant to give you somewhere to stand while you keep praying, keep seeking, keep building a life in the very place that doesn't feel like home yet — because a few verses earlier, God had already told them to plant gardens and build houses in Babylon rather than simply waiting it out (Jeremiah 29:5-7). Believing the end is secure is exactly what frees you to live faithfully in the middle.

"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose."Romans 8:28, KJV

Paul isn't saying every individual thing that happens to you is good. He's saying the same thing Jeremiah said to Judah: the Author is still writing, the ending is still peace, and what looks like a detour to you is still inside the plan to Him.

The Word for Today

If you've been bracing for the worst, quietly assuming the long wait means God changed His mind about you — hear what He told a nation in exile and never once retracted: I know the thoughts I think toward you. Not evil. Not punishment. Not abandonment. Peace, and an expected end. The seventy years were never a sign He'd forgotten the promise. They were the promise, being kept one faithful day at a time, right up until the ending He already wrote arrives exactly on schedule.
