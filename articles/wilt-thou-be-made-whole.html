Wilt Thou Be Made Whole?

Thirty-eight years by the pool, and somebody always reached the water first. Jesus stood over his mat and asked a question that sounds almost rude — wilt thou be made whole? — then bypassed the water entirely: Rise, take up thy bed, and walk.

Word and Faith Network · John 5:6-8
"When Jesus saw him lie, and knew that he had been now a long time in that case, he saith unto him, Wilt thou be made whole? The impotent man answered him, Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me. Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk."
John 5:6-8, KJV

Some things stop feeling like a crisis. They start feeling like furniture. The pain you've had so long you rearranged your life around it. The habit you quit fighting. The loneliness you've learned to call "just my personality." The marriage that went quiet years ago. The dream you folded up and slid under the bed. It doesn't hurt like it used to — and that's the problem. You've gotten used to it. If that's you this Tuesday, I want to take you to a pool in Jerusalem called Bethesda, five porches full of hurting people, one man who had been lying there longer than some of them had been alive, and the strangest question Jesus ever asked: Wilt thou be made whole?

Thirty and Eight Years

John paints the scene with no softness at all. By the sheep gate there was a pool, and around it "a great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, withered, waiting for the moving of the water." A hospital with no doctors. A waiting room with no appointments. And somewhere in that crowd, "a certain man... which had an infirmity thirty and eight years."

Thirty-eight years. Sit with that number. That's not a bad season. That's a whole adult life. Israel spent thirty-eight years wandering after Kadeshbarnea — this man spent the same stretch on a mat. Somewhere in those years, waiting stopped being temporary and became an address. People stopped asking how he was doing. They just knew where to find him.

Wilt Thou Be Made Whole?

Then Jesus walks in. Past the porches. Past the multitude. And the Bible says, "When Jesus saw him lie, and knew that he had been now a long time in that case..." Stop right there. Before the man said one word, Jesus saw him. And Jesus knew. Nobody had to brief Him. The heaven you thought forgot you has been counting every one of those years.

Then comes the question: "Wilt thou be made whole?" It sounds almost rude. Of course he wants to be healed — he's lying at a healing pool! But Jesus never asks a question because He's short on information. He asks because you need to hear your own answer. After thirty-eight years, a man can stop wanting. Not out loud. Quietly. Hope gets expensive, so you stop paying for it. And wholeness costs something — a new routine, new responsibility, no more excuse, no more spot by the pool. Some people aren't afraid of staying sick. They're afraid of getting well.

So here's my question for you today: do you still want it? Not "do you deserve it." Not "do you think it's possible." Do you want it? Because Jesus will not drag you into a wholeness you've decided you can live without.

Sir, I Have No Man

Listen to his answer — because he never says yes. He says, "Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me." Jesus asked about his will, and he answered with his network. No help. No connections. Always too slow. Somebody always gets there first.

Some of you could recite that sentence in your sleep. I have no man. No uncle at the company. No one in the family who's done it before. Nobody to vouch for you, lift you, carry you to the edge. You've watched other people get the job, the spouse, the breakthrough, the miracle — while you're still crawling toward the water. And a lie settles in: blessing is for people with connections, and I don't have any.

"When my father and my mother forsake me, then the LORD will take me up."Psalm 27:10, KJV

Here's the holy irony. He said "I have no man" to the only Man who ever mattered. He was explaining why he couldn't reach the water to the One who walks on it. You don't need someone to lower you into the pool when the Healer of the pool is standing over your mat. You may have no man. But you have the Son of Man — and He doesn't need the water to move.

Rise, Take Up Thy Bed

Jesus doesn't argue with the excuse. He doesn't organize a team to carry him to the water next time it stirs. He bypasses the whole system with three commands: "Rise, take up thy bed, and walk." Rise — do the thing you couldn't do. Take up thy bed — pick up what used to hold you down. Walk — go somewhere you've never been.

And notice: take up thy bed. Not "leave it there just in case this doesn't work." God doesn't want you keeping a backup plan for your bondage. That mat had carried him for thirty-eight years. Now he would carry it. What used to carry you is about to be carried by you — not as a burden, but as a receipt. The thing that defined you becomes the proof of what He did.

"But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed."Isaiah 53:5, KJV

Immediately

Verse nine: "And immediately the man was made whole, and took up his bed, and walked." Thirty-eight years of waiting. One sentence from Jesus. Immediately. The length of your wait does not set the length of your deliverance. What took decades to settle in can leave in a moment when He speaks. The healing was never in the water. It was always in Him.

But watch where the miracle met him — on the way up. He didn't wait to feel strong before he tried to rise. He tried to rise, and the strength showed up halfway. Faith is not feeling healed and then standing. Faith is standing because He said so.

So answer Him today — out loud, on purpose. "Yes, Lord. I will be made whole." Stop rehearsing who didn't help you. Stop measuring your chances by the crowd at the pool. Fold the mat. Put your feet on the floor. And say it before the old excuse can clear its throat: "I have no man — but I have Jesus. I rise, I take up my bed, and I walk." Your thirty-eight years are not the end of your story. They're the length of the testimony you're about to tell. In Jesus' name.
