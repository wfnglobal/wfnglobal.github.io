Ye Ask, and Receive Not

Not every unanswered prayer is a closed door. Some are a mirror.

Word and Faith Network · James 4
"Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts."
James 4:3 — KJV

James does not soften this verse, and we should resist the urge to soften it for him. He is writing to believers — people who prayed, and did not receive, and apparently assumed the fault was God's. James corrects that assumption directly: the problem was not that God withheld. The problem was how, and why, they asked.

This is not a comfortable teaching. It is far easier to believe that an unanswered prayer simply means "not yet," or "that wasn't God's will," than to ask whether the prayer itself was asked amiss. But James puts that question in front of us, and faith that is honest with scripture has to sit with it.

The Verse Before the Verse

Context matters here. The verse right before this one says: "Ye lust, and have not: ye kill, and desire to have, and cannot obtain: ye fight and war, yet ye have not, because ye ask not" (v.2). James is describing a community consumed by unmet desire — wanting, striving, competing, and still coming up empty. Some of them, he says, don't have because they never ask at all. Others ask, and still don't receive — and it's this second group verse 3 addresses.

"Ye adulterers and adulteresses, know ye not that the friendship of the world is enmity with God?"James 4:4, KJV

The surrounding passage makes clear what kind of "asking amiss" James has in view: prayer entangled with worldly appetite, requesting from God what is really meant to feed the flesh rather than serve His purposes.

What "Amiss" Actually Means

The Greek word translated "amiss" is kakos — wrongly, badly, with a corrupt motive. This is not about imperfect grammar in prayer, or failing to use the right formula. It is about the why behind the ask. "That ye may consume it upon your lusts" — the request was never really about glorifying God or advancing His kingdom. It was about self-indulgence wearing the language of prayer.

This matters because it means the issue is rarely the size of the request. A believer can pray boldly and biblically for provision, healing, breakthrough, or increase — and receive, because the heart behind it is aligned with God's purposes. Another can pray for the very same things with a heart bent toward self-gratification alone, and find heaven strangely silent.

Praying According to His Will

This verse is not a reason to pray timidly or to assume every desire is suspect. Scripture is clear that God delights to give good gifts to His children (Matthew 7:11), and that we may "ask any thing according to his will" with confidence of being heard (1 John 5:14). The correction is not against asking boldly — it is against asking selfishly.

"But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you."Matthew 6:33, KJV

When the kingdom comes first in a believer's heart, the asking changes shape. It is no longer "give me this so I can consume it on myself," but "give me this so Your purpose is served through my life." The same request can be asked two different ways — and James says heaven can tell the difference.

The Word for Today

If there is a prayer you have carried for a long time without answer, this is not an invitation to despair — it is an invitation to examine. Not to doubt that God hears, but to ask honestly what is really behind the request. Align the ask with His purposes, and pray boldly, expectantly, and rightly. The throne of grace is open (Hebrews 4:16) — come to it asking well.
