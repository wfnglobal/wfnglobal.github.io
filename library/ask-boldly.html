Ask Boldly

Why Some Prayers Move Heaven and Others Don't

Word and Faith Network · 8 Chapters · ~18 min read
Begin Reading → All Books

There is a question underneath every unanswered prayer, and most of us never quite let ourselves ask it out loud: why did that one work, and this one didn't? We have all prayed prayers that seemed to move heaven itself — and others that felt like they never left the room.

This book did not start as a book. It started as a series of daily studies — Hebrews 4:16, James 4:2-3, James 1:5 — each one answering a piece of the same question from a different angle. Put together, they form something larger than any single devotional: a real, honest look at what scripture actually says about why some prayers move heaven, and why others don't.

This is not a formula. God is not a vending machine, and faith is not a technique for extracting outcomes from a reluctant deity. But scripture is not silent on this either. James tells us plainly that some prayers go unanswered for reasons we can actually examine. Hebrews tells us just as plainly that the door to the throne of grace is already open. Both are true at once, and learning to hold them together is what this book is about.

Eight chapters, each built on one passage, each one answering a different piece of the question. Read a chapter a day, or read it in one sitting — either way, come ready to examine your own praying, not just your own believing.

Chapter One

The Door Is Already Open

"Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need."
Hebrews 4:16 — KJV

Before we examine why some prayers go unanswered, we have to settle something more basic: are we even allowed to come? Many believers pray from a kind of permanent distance, as if every prayer is a formal petition to a reluctant monarch who might, if the timing and wording are right, choose to respond. Hebrews 4:16 destroys that picture in a single verse.

"Let us therefore" — the verse begins with a conclusion, drawn from everything just said about Jesus as our great High Priest, who was tempted in every way as we are, yet without sin (v.15). Because He sympathizes with our weakness and has already made the way, we are told to come — not timidly, not hoping, but boldly. The Greek word is parrhesia: freedom of speech, confidence, the unguarded candor of a child in a safe home, not the careful formality of a stranger at a gate.

"Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus."Hebrews 10:19, KJV

This matters more than it might first appear, because the posture we bring to prayer shapes the prayer itself. A believer who approaches God expecting to be turned away prays cautiously, vaguely, defensively — hedging every request so the inevitable "no" won't sting as much. A believer who knows the door is already open prays specifically, expectantly, honestly. The first posture asks small because it does not trust the welcome. The second asks boldly because the welcome has already been settled at the cross.

Notice, too, what we are told we will find when we come: "mercy" and "grace to help in time of need." Not interrogation. Not a probation period to prove our worthiness. Mercy for what we've done wrong, and grace — God's unearned strength — for what we cannot do on our own. This is the throne of grace, not the bench of judgment.

So before we go any further into why some prayers seem to fail, hold onto this first: the failure, when it happens, is never because the door was closed. The door was nailed open at Calvary. Whatever we discover in the chapters ahead about asking rightly, it is correction for people already welcome in the room — not a new set of locks on the door.

Chapter Two

Ye Have Not, Because Ye Ask Not

"Ye lust, and have not: ye kill, and desire to have, and cannot obtain: ye fight and war, yet ye have not, because ye ask not."
James 4:2 — KJV

If the door is already open, as chapter one established, then the simplest explanation for an empty-handed life is sometimes the most overlooked: we never actually knocked. James paints a vivid, almost exhausting picture — people lusting, striving, fighting, warring for what they want, and still coming up empty. Not because God withheld it. Because they never asked for it at all.

This is worth sitting with, because most of us would rather believe almost anything else about our unmet needs than this: that we tried everything except prayer. We planned. We worked. We worried. We maneuvered relationships and circumstances to try to engineer the outcome ourselves. And the whole time, the one resource available to us — direct access to the Father who already said He gives liberally (James 1:5) — sat unused.

"Ye have not, because ye ask not."James 4:2, KJV

Why do we skip it? Partly pride — asking means admitting we cannot produce the outcome ourselves, and that admission costs something. Partly habit — we have trained ourselves to solve problems with effort long before we think to bring them to God. And partly, if we're honest, a quiet unbelief: a suspicion that asking won't actually change anything, so why bother.

James's answer to all three is simply: ask. Not as a last resort after every other strategy has failed, but as the first move. "In every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God" (Philippians 4:6). Not after you've exhausted your own strength — in every thing, from the start.

This chapter is short because the lesson is simple, even if it isn't easy: before you examine your motives, before you refine your theology of prayer, before you wonder whether you're asking correctly — make sure you're actually asking. An unprayed request cannot be answered. The empty hand that never reaches out cannot be filled.

Chapter Three

Ask Amiss

"Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts."
James 4:3 — KJV

James does not stop at "you never asked." He goes further, to a harder diagnosis: some of his readers did ask, and still did not receive — because of how, and why, they asked. "Ye ask amiss" uses a Greek word meaning to miss the mark, to aim badly. It is not that the request itself was forbidden. It is that the aim behind it was wrong.

"That ye may consume it upon your lusts" names the target precisely: these were prayers aimed entirely at self-gratification, with God treated as a means to a private end rather than the end Himself. This is the uncomfortable mirror this chapter holds up. It is entirely possible to pray biblically worded requests — for provision, for breakthrough, for increase — with a heart bent completely toward self rather than toward Him.

"But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you."Matthew 6:33, KJV

Notice what this is not. It is not a reason to feel guilty for wanting things, or to spiritualize every desire into suspicion. God delights to give good gifts to His children (Matthew 7:11), and plenty of our ordinary needs — provision, healing, direction — are exactly the kind of things He invites us to bring Him. The issue James names is narrower and more specific: prayer that exists only to feed appetite, with no room left for God's purposes at all.

The test, then, is not "is this request too big" or "is this request too selfish-sounding." The test is: if this is granted, whose kingdom grows? A believer can ask for the exact same thing — a business to prosper, a dream to be realized — from two entirely different hearts. One asks so it can be consumed alone. The other asks so that what God gives can overflow to others and bring Him glory. James says heaven can tell the difference, even when our own words sound identical.

This is not a call to pray smaller. It is a call to examine, honestly, what is sitting underneath the request — and to let that examination shape not whether we ask, but how.

Chapter Four

The Wisdom Prayer

"If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him."
James 1:5 — KJV

After naming the ways prayer can go wrong, James offers one of the most reassuring promises in all of scripture — and it is worth noticing how simply it is stated. No conditions about worthiness. No warning about asking amiss. Just: if you lack wisdom, ask, and it will be given.

Why does wisdom get this uncomplicated promise, when other requests seem to require so much more careful examination? Because wisdom is never a selfish request. Nobody asks for the ability to see clearly, to judge rightly, to know what to do, purely to consume it on their own appetites. Wisdom, by its nature, is aimed outward — toward a decision, a relationship, a situation that needs clarity. It is one of the few requests that is almost always asked amiss-free.

"The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding."Proverbs 9:10, KJV

Two words in this verse deserve special attention. "Liberally" — the Greek haplos, meaning simply, generously, without a hidden agenda. God does not dole out wisdom in stingy portions to the already-impressive and withhold it from the confused. And "upbraideth not" — He does not scold you for needing to ask again. You can come back to this well as many times as the need arises, and you will never be met with "I already told you this" or "shouldn't you know this by now." He gives, simply and repeatedly, to the one who keeps coming.

There is a quiet correction embedded here too, for those of us who try to think our way to every answer before we pray about it. James does not say "figure it out, then pray about your plan." He says: if you lack it, ask for it — as a first resource, not a last one, the same lesson chapter two already taught us about every other need.

If chapter three warned us to examine our motives before we ask, this chapter hands us one request we never need to examine twice. Lacking wisdom is not a failure to confess carefully — it is simply the truth, and God has already promised what He'll do with it.

Chapter Five

According to His Will

"And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us: and if we know that he hear us, whatsoever we ask, we know that we have the petitions that we desired of him."
1 John 5:14-15 — KJV

By now a pattern has emerged across these chapters: boldness is not the problem, and prayer itself is never discouraged — what matters is alignment. 1 John 5:14-15 names that alignment directly: confidence in prayer is tied to asking "according to his will." This single phrase reframes everything we've covered so far.

It is tempting to hear "according to his will" as a dampener — a polite hedge added to every prayer just in case God disagrees with us. But that is not how John presents it. He presents it as the very source of our confidence, not a qualifier on it. Knowing God's will does not shrink our boldness; it is what makes bold, specific, expectant prayer possible in the first place.

"And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart."Jeremiah 29:13, KJV

How do we know God's will well enough to pray this way? Largely through what He has already revealed. Scripture is full of His stated purposes — that none should perish (2 Peter 3:9), that His people walk in wisdom and holiness, that His kingdom advance, that we bear fruit, that the sick be prayed for in faith (James 5:15). The more our minds are filled with what God has already said He wants, the more our prayers naturally align with it, almost without having to strain for the right words.

This is also where chapter three's warning and this chapter's promise meet. "Asking amiss" (James 4:3) and "asking according to his will" (1 John 5:14) are two descriptions of the same line, seen from opposite sides. One is prayer aimed at self; the other is prayer shaped by what God has already revealed He wants. The same request — healing, provision, breakthrough — can be prayed from either posture. Alignment is what moves it from one side of that line to the other.

And once we are praying from that alignment, verse 15 makes an extraordinary claim: "we know that we have the petitions that we desired of him." Not someday, in theory — have, present tense. Prayer rooted in God's revealed will is not a hopeful guess thrown toward heaven. It is a transaction already settled, waiting only to be seen.

Chapter Six

The Prayer That Believes

"Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them."
Mark 11:24 — KJV

Alignment with God's will, covered in the last chapter, answers the question of what to pray. This chapter addresses how to pray it — and Mark 11:24 is one of the boldest statements Jesus makes about prayer anywhere in the Gospels. Believe that you receive, He says, and you will have it. Not believe that you might receive. Not hope you'll receive. Believe, as a settled fact, before the evidence has arrived.

This verse sits directly after Jesus curses a fig tree and it withers by the next day — a dramatic, visible demonstration of exactly this principle in action. The disciples marvel at the speed of it, and Jesus uses the moment to teach them that the same kind of faith is available to them, not just to Him.

"Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him... And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up."James 5:14-15, KJV

Notice the sequencing in Mark 11:24: believe first, receive second. This runs against our natural instinct, which wants proof before faith — evidence first, belief second. Scripture consistently reverses that order. Faith is "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1) — which means faith does not wait for the evidence to exist. It is itself the substance, before the visible proof catches up.

This is not positive thinking dressed in Christian language. The faith Jesus describes here is not a mental trick for manufacturing confidence — it is the natural response of someone who has actually believed God's word about the matter. Which is why chapter five's emphasis on alignment matters so much here: praying "believing you receive" for something outside God's revealed will is presumption, not faith. But praying that way for something scripture has already promised — healing, provision, wisdom, peace — is simply taking God at His word before the physical world has caught up to it.

This is often where prayer quietly fails, even after every other box has been checked: the request is rightly aimed, rightly motivated, rightly aligned with God's will — and then abandoned to doubt the moment it leaves our lips. Mark 11:24 calls us to something harder than asking well. It calls us to keep believing after we've asked, until what we've believed becomes what we see.

Chapter Seven

When the Answer Delays

"And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint."
Luke 18:1 — KJV

Everything covered so far — boldness, honest asking, right motive, alignment, believing prayer — still leaves one real experience unaddressed: the prayer that is asked rightly, believed sincerely, and still does not seem to be answered today. What then? Luke 18 answers with a parable, not a formula: the persistent widow, who keeps coming to an unjust judge until he finally grants her request — not because he cares, but because her persistence wears him down.

Jesus's point is not that God is reluctant like the unjust judge — He makes that explicit: "shall not God avenge his own elect, which cry day and night unto him, though he bear long with them?" (v.7). The comparison runs the opposite direction: if even an uncaring judge eventually yields to persistence, how much more will a loving Father respond to His own children who keep coming to Him?

"And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not."Galatians 6:9, KJV

A delay is not the same thing as a denial, even though it can feel identical from the inside. Scripture is full of answered prayers that took time — Daniel's request answered on day one in heaven, but delayed twenty-one days by unseen resistance before he ever saw it (Daniel 10:12-13); Abraham waiting decades for the son he was promised; Hannah's years of prayer before Samuel was born. In each case, the prayer was heard immediately. The visible answer simply had a timeline the one praying could not see.

This is where "pray and not faint" becomes the actual discipline of mature faith. Fainting is not giving up on God existing — it is quietly giving up on the specific request, letting it go cold, deciding the delay means no. Persistence, by contrast, keeps bringing the same request back to the same throne, not out of doubt that it was heard the first time, but out of confidence that the answer is in motion even when it is not yet visible.

If your prayer has not yet arrived, this chapter is not a suggestion to examine your faith for defects. It may simply be a season to keep asking — not because God is unmoved, but because something unseen is already moving, and your continued prayer is part of how it arrives.

Chapter Eight

Boldness That Stays Humble

"Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus."
Philippians 4:6-7 — KJV

Seven chapters in, we have covered a door that is already open, a failure to ask at all, a failure to ask rightly, a promise with no conditions, the difference alignment makes, the call to believe before we see, and the discipline of persistence when the answer delays. It would be easy to walk away from all of this with a kind of anxious checklist: am I bold enough? Humble enough? Aligned enough? Believing enough? Patient enough?

Philippians 4:6-7 gathers everything into something simpler, and it starts in an unexpected place: not with technique, but with release. "Be careful for nothing" — literally, be anxious for nothing. Before Paul ever gets to the instruction to pray, he addresses the posture underneath it. Prayer offered from anxiety is not the boldness Hebrews 4:16 describes; it is striving wearing prayer's clothing.

"Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you."1 Peter 5:7, KJV

True boldness and true humility are not opposites — they are the same posture, viewed from two sides. Boldness says: I have every right to come to this throne, because Christ has made the way. Humility says: and I bring nothing to demand, only requests to entrust. Put them together, and you get exactly what Paul describes — requests made known "with thanksgiving," confident enough to ask specifically, surrendered enough to release the outcome.

This is the real answer to the question this book opened with: why do some prayers move heaven, and others don't? Not because God is inconsistent, and not because faith is a formula to be perfected. It is because prayer is a relationship, and like any relationship, it is shaped by honesty, right motive, trust, and endurance. The believer who comes boldly, asks honestly, aligns with what God has revealed, believes without wavering, and keeps coming back without fainting — that believer has not found a trick. They have simply learned to pray the way scripture has been describing all along.

The door was never the problem. It was nailed open two thousand years ago, and it has not shut since. Whatever stands between you and the answer you're waiting for, it is not God's willingness. Come boldly. Ask rightly. Believe what you've asked. And do not faint before the answer arrives — it is already on its way.
