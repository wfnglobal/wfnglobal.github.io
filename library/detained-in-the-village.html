For the brother from New York, who gave this book its story and its title, and for everyone still tied in the village, waiting to hear the Lord say, "Loose him."

Twenty years. That is how long he had lived in New York. Twenty winters of early trains and double shifts. Twenty years of sending money home, starting over, and watching people who arrived after him buy houses, open businesses and move on. He was not lazy. He was not foolish. Every door he knocked on opened just wide enough for him to see what he could not have. His body was in America. His life, he would later learn, had never left the village.

Then he met a man of God. The pastor prayed with him, and in prayer the Lord showed him what he had never considered: he was being held back in his village, through a member of his own family. Not bad luck. Not the economy. He was detained.

Most of us would have said "Amen" and gone home. This pastor bought a ticket. He travelled with him back to Nigeria. When they reached the village, they did not go straight to the family compound. They checked into a hotel and waited on God.

At midnight the pastor woke him. "Get up. Someone powerful is summoning your spirit tonight. They mean to kill you before morning." There was no time to be afraid. The two men prayed through midnight, through the small hours, through every hour the enemy had chosen, until daybreak. The psalmist said the enemy "sitteth in the lurking places of the villages" (Psalm 10:8). That night the lurking place was exposed.

In the morning they met an elderly woman in his family. The pastor did not raise a hand against her or shame her before the village. He spoke with the authority of Jesus to the power at work through her, and commanded that the man be released from the shrine where his life had been held. Loose him, and let him go.

He was let go. The man who had nothing to show for twenty years became a successful businessman. More than that, he gave his life to Jesus Christ, and today he is a pastor in his church: the captive now proclaiming liberty to the captives.

This book began with his story. When he shared it with me, he even gave me its title: Detained in the Village. I could not let it go. I went digging in the Scriptures to see what God says about the village, and I found it everywhere: an enemy lurking in the villages, a colt tied in a village, a blind man led out of a village, a funeral stopped at a village gate. What I found is in these pages.

Now hear me carefully, because this is where many people go wrong. Most deliverance does not look like this. Jesus opened Bartimaeus's eyes with a word (Mark 10:52). He put clay on another man's eyes and sent him to wash (John 9:6-7). He touched a third man twice (Mark 8:23-25). He will not be confined to one method. You may never board a plane. You may never learn who or what stood against you, and you do not need to. Do not go looking for someone in your family to blame. Go looking for Jesus. What is guaranteed is not the method but the result: "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed" (John 8:36).

This book is for everyone whose address changed but whose story did not. You left the village. You crossed rivers, borders, oceans. You work hard, pray hard and give hard, yet the same ceiling your father hit is waiting for you in a new country. The same pattern that broke your uncle's marriage is knocking at yours. The same "almost" that followed your mother follows you. Your body is in Lagos, London, Houston or Toronto. Something of your life is still tied to a post in the village.

Eight chapters walk through four passages the Holy Spirit has laid on my heart: the enemy who lurks in the villages (Psalm 10), the colt tied in the village where two ways met (Mark 11), the blind man Jesus led out of the village (Mark 8), and the funeral Jesus stopped at the village gate (Luke 7). At the end you will find the Freedom Section: seven steps, a prophetic deliverance prayer, and a page to write down your answer when it comes. It will come. Keep praying. Do not give up. The village had its night. The morning belongs to the Lord.

Chapter One

The Lurking Places of the Villages

"He sitteth in the lurking places of the villages: in the secret places doth he murder the innocent: his eyes are privily set against the poor. He lieth in wait secretly as a lion in his den: he lieth in wait to catch the poor: he doth catch the poor, when he draweth him into his net. He croucheth, and humbleth himself, that the poor may fall by his strong ones."
Psalm 10:8-10 — KJV

David did not write this psalm about a monster in a faraway forest. He wrote it about the village. The Hebrew word for "villages" here describes an enclosed courtyard, a settlement fenced in, the compound people call home. And the word for "lurking places" means an ambush. Put them together and you have the most unsettling picture in the Psalms: an enemy hiding inside the fence, sitting quietly in the very place you were supposed to be safe.

Read his tactics slowly, because the enemy has not changed his methods in three thousand years. He sits. He watches "privily," in secret. He lies in wait "as a lion in his den." He draws the poor "into his net." And then, the strangest detail of all: "He croucheth, and humbleth himself." The thing that is fighting you rarely looks strong. It crouches. It smiles. It greets you at Christmas. It asks how the job is going over there. It looks harmless, and that is exactly how the poor "fall by his strong ones."

Some of you have felt this and could not name it. You were the first in your family to finish school, and then everything began to slip. You travelled out with great hope, and twenty years later you have papers but no peace, a salary but no savings, a marriage but no rest. Every time you rise, something pulls you back to the same spot. You are not imagining it. Scripture describes it. There are lurking places in the villages.

"Ought not this woman, being a daughter of Abraham, whom Satan hath bound, lo, these eighteen years, be loosed from this bond?"Luke 13:16, KJV

Jesus Himself named it. When He healed a woman who had been bent double for eighteen years, He did not call it bad posture. He said Satan had bound her. And He did not stop at the diagnosis. He loosed her, in the synagogue, on the Sabbath, in front of every religious person who preferred she stay bent. That is the pattern of this whole book. We will name the bondage honestly. But we will never stop at the name. We go on to the loosing.

Because here is what most people miss in Psalm 10: the psalm does not end in the ambush. It turns. "Arise, O LORD; O God, lift up thine hand: forget not the humble" (v.12). "Thou hast seen it" (v.14). Whatever has been watching you privily, God has been watching it. Every secret meeting held about your life, He has seen. "Thou art the helper of the fatherless" (v.14). And then the verse that settles everything: "The LORD is King for ever and ever" (v.16). Not the village. Not the shrine. Not the oldest person in the family. The LORD is King.

The psalm ends with God arising "to judge the fatherless and the oppressed, that the man of the earth may no more oppress" (v.18). No more. That is the promise over your life as you open this book. The lurking place has been a secret long enough. The Lord is lifting up His hand.

So start where David started: be honest. Tell God what has been happening. You do not need to know who is behind it. You only need to know Who is above it.

Pray this

  • Arise, O LORD, and lift up Your hand over my life. Expose every lurking place set against me (Psalm 10:12).
  • Every net spread for my feet in my village, break, in the name of Jesus (Psalm 124:7).
  • Lord, You have seen it. Judge my case and let the oppressor oppress no more (Psalm 10:14, 18).
  • I declare that the LORD is King over my life, my family and my future, for ever and ever.

Chapter Two

Your Body Left, Your Life Stayed

"And Terah took Abram his son... and they went forth with them from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of Canaan; and they came unto Haran, and dwelt there... and Terah died in Haran."
Genesis 11:31-32 — KJV

Before God ever called Abram, his father Terah had already set out for Canaan. Read it again: "to go into the land of Canaan." Terah had the right destination. He packed the family. He left Ur. And then he "came unto Haran, and dwelt there." He stopped halfway. He settled in a place that was not home anymore and not yet the promise. And the Bible gives him one sad line as an epitaph: "Terah died in Haran."

That is the story of so many of God's people today. You left. You really did leave. You have the passport stamps to prove it. But somewhere between the village and the promise, you settled in a Haran: a place where the struggle is familiar, where the ceiling feels normal, where you stopped expecting more. You are not where you started, but you are not where God was taking you either.

And Haran is dangerous because it looks like progress. You moved. You are working. People back home think you have made it. Only you know that you are still paying the same bills to the same patterns, just in a different currency.

"But his wife looked back from behind him, and she became a pillar of salt."Genesis 19:26, KJV

Then there is Lot's wife. The angels did not just tell Lot's family to leave Sodom. They took them by the hand and pulled them out (Genesis 19:16). "Escape for thy life; look not behind thee" (v.17). Her feet obeyed. Her body was out of the city. But her heart turned around. She looked back, and she became a pillar of salt, frozen forever at the border between where she had been and where she was supposed to go.

Jesus thought that story was so important that He compressed it into three words: "Remember Lot's wife" (Luke 17:32). Why? Because it is possible to be physically delivered and still spiritually detained. It is possible to be out of the village and still answering to it.

How does the heart look back? Sometimes through fear: "If I prosper, they will come for me." Sometimes through guilt: "Who am I to rise when they are still suffering?" Sometimes through old agreements: a vow you did not make, a dedication you did not choose, an altar that still has your name on it. Sometimes through words that were spoken over you as a child and never broken: "You will never amount to anything." "Nobody in this family ever finishes." Those words have travelled further than you think. They have followed you through immigration without a visa.

The writer of Hebrews explains why some people end up back where they started: "if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country" (Hebrews 11:15-16). What your mind keeps returning to, your life eventually follows. So the battle is not only in the village. It is in your mind, your expectations and your agreements.

Here is the good news. Abram did not die in Haran. When Terah's journey ended, God's call began: "Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee" (Genesis 12:1). And Abram went. The halfway place does not have to be your final address. The God who called Abram is calling you to finish the journey.

Pray this

  • Father, I refuse to settle in Haran. Take me all the way into the land You promised (Genesis 12:1).
  • Every part of my heart still looking back to the village, Lord, turn it toward You.
  • Every word spoken over me as a child that is holding my life in place, I break it now by the word of God (Proverbs 18:21).
  • I will not become a pillar at the border. I go forward in Jesus' name.

Chapter Three

Tied Where Two Ways Met

"Go your way into the village over against you: and as soon as ye be entered into it, ye shall find a colt tied, whereon never man sat; loose him, and bring him. And if any man say unto you, Why do ye this? say ye that the Lord hath need of him."
Mark 11:2-3 — KJV

Jesus was about to ride into Jerusalem as King. Crowds were waiting with palm branches. Prophecy was about to be fulfilled to the letter (Zechariah 9:9). And the animal He needed for the most important ride in history was standing in a village, tied up.

Look at what the disciples found: "the colt tied by the door without in a place where two ways met" (Mark 11:4). Every word is a sermon. Tied: it had the strength to walk but could not move. By the door: it was right at the threshold, close enough to see out, never allowed through. Where two ways met: it was standing at a crossroads, at the very spot where a decision could be made, and it could not take either road.

Some of you have lived your whole adult life by the door. You have stood at the threshold of marriage, of a business, of ministry, of a breakthrough, so many times that you could describe the door in detail. You know what is on the other side. You have just never been allowed to walk through it. You are not lacking strength. You are tied.

"Whereon never man sat."Mark 11:2, KJV

And notice: "whereon never man sat." This colt had never been used. It had never carried anything. It was young, strong and full of purpose, and it had never once done what it was made to do. That is what village bondage steals from people. Not always their life, but their usefulness. The gift is there. The calling is there. The potential is obvious to everyone. It has just never carried anything.

Then Jesus gave His disciples two words that I want you to hear over your own life: "loose him." Not "pray about whether he should be loosed." Not "wait until the owners agree." Loose him. Jesus sent His people to the village with authority to untie what had been tied.

And as soon as they began, someone challenged them: "What do ye, loosing the colt?" (v.5). Mark it down: whenever God starts to loose you, someone will ask why. A relative will question your new direction. A voice will tell you, "This is how it has always been in our family." Your own mind will ask, "Who do you think you are?" Jesus prepared His disciples for that moment with one answer: "The Lord hath need of him."

That is your answer too. You are not breaking free to show off. You are not loosed so you can be proud. You are loosed because the Lord has need of you. He has a road for you to walk and a King for you to carry into a city. And when the Lord says He needs something, no rope in any village has the right to hold it. The disciples "loose him," and the colt that had never been ridden carried Jesus into Jerusalem with the crowd shouting "Hosanna" (Mark 11:7-9).

You were not made to stand by the door forever. The Lord has need of you.

Pray this

  • Every rope tying my destiny by the door where two ways meet, be loosed now in the name of Jesus (Mark 11:4).
  • The Lord has need of me. I am released to carry what He made me to carry.
  • Every voice asking "Why do ye this?" over my freedom, be silenced by the word of the Lord.
  • Lord, let the gift in me that has never been used begin to carry Your glory.

Chapter Four

Sour Grapes No More

"What mean ye, that ye use this proverb concerning the land of Israel, saying, The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge? As I live, saith the Lord GOD, ye shall not have occasion any more to use this proverb in Israel. Behold, all souls are mine."
Ezekiel 18:2-4 — KJV

Every village has its proverbs. Some are wise. Some are poison. Israel had one that sounded like wisdom but worked like a chain: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge." In other words: your parents made the choices, so you live with the taste. Your grandfather made the covenant, so you pay the debt. Whatever happened before you were born decides what happens to you.

Many of us grew up under that proverb, even if we never heard it in those words. "In this family, the men die young." "Our women don't stay married." "Nobody from our compound ever prospers abroad." It is said with a sigh, as if it were weather. And because everyone says it, everyone believes it, and because everyone believes it, everyone lives it.

Then God interrupts. "As I live, saith the Lord GOD, ye shall not have occasion any more to use this proverb." He does not adjust the proverb. He bans it. And He gives the reason in five words: "Behold, all souls are mine." Your soul does not belong to your village. It does not belong to your father's covenants or your mother's shrine. It belongs to God.

Now, the Bible does not pretend that families have no influence. God told Moses He visits "the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me" (Exodus 20:5). Patterns are real. Sin leaves tracks. But read the next verse, the one people forget to quote: "And shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments" (Exodus 20:6). Three or four generations of judgment against thousands of generations of mercy. The curse has a short reach. Mercy has a long arm.

"Ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold, from your vain conversation received by tradition from your fathers; but with the precious blood of Christ."1 Peter 1:18-19, KJV

This is the key verse of this book. Peter was writing to people who had inherited empty ways of living from their fathers: traditions, idols, patterns, practices. And he told them they had been redeemed out of it. Redeemed is a market word. It means bought back. Somebody had a claim on you, and Christ paid the price that cancelled it. Not with money. Not with a ritual. "With the precious blood of Christ."

Paul says the same thing from another angle: "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). Whatever curse was legally yours, Jesus took it onto Himself on the cross. And to the Colossians: He blotted out "the handwriting of ordinances that was against us... nailing it to his cross; and having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly" (Colossians 2:14-15). Whatever paperwork the village holds against you has been torn up and nailed to a tree.

And there is more. You were not only bought out of one family. You were adopted into another. "Ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father" (Romans 8:15). You have a new Father. A new name. A new inheritance. When the village calls you by your old family's fears, heaven calls you by your Father's name.

So stop using the proverb. Stop saying, "It runs in our family." God has said you shall not have occasion to use it any more. Say instead what Balaam was forced to say over Israel: "Surely there is no enchantment against Jacob, neither is there any divination against Israel" (Numbers 23:23). You are not a product of sour grapes. You are a child of the King.

Pray this

  • By the blood of Jesus, I cancel every proverb of failure spoken over my family line (Ezekiel 18:3).
  • I am redeemed from every empty way received from my fathers, by the precious blood of Christ (1 Peter 1:18-19).
  • Every handwriting written against me in my village, be blotted out and nailed to the cross (Colossians 2:14).
  • Abba, Father, I receive my new name and my new inheritance as Your child (Romans 8:15-17).

Chapter Five

Throw Down Your Father's Altar

"And it came to pass the same night, that the LORD said unto him, Take thy father's young bullock... and throw down the altar of Baal that thy father hath, and cut down the grove that is by it: and build an altar unto the LORD thy God."
Judges 6:25-26 — KJV

Gideon had just been called a "mighty man of valour" by an angel (Judges 6:12). God had promised him victory over the Midianites who had been stripping Israel bare for seven years. You would expect his first assignment to be the battlefield. It was not. His first assignment was his father's backyard.

"Throw down the altar of Baal that thy father hath." Before Gideon could deliver a nation, he had to deal with what was standing in his own family compound. There was an altar there, an agreement with a false god, and a grove beside it. As long as it stood, Israel kept losing its harvest. Some battles are lost in the field because they were never fought at home.

Gideon obeyed, but notice how: "because he feared his father's household, and the men of the city, that he could not do it by day, that he did it by night" (v.27). I love the honesty of that verse. He was afraid. He did it anyway. Courage is not the absence of fear. It is obedience with your knees shaking.

In the morning, the town woke up to a broken altar, and they wanted blood: "Bring out thy son, that he may die" (v.30). This is what happens when you deal with the village altar. The village notices. But then something remarkable happened. Gideon's own father, Joash, the man who owned the altar, stood up for his son: "Will ye plead for Baal?... if he be a god, let him plead for himself" (v.31). The father whose altar was destroyed became the son's defender. When you obey God at home, He can turn even your family around.

"Oh that thou wouldest bless me indeed, and enlarge my coast, and that thine hand might be with me, and that thou wouldest keep me from evil, that it may not grieve me! And God granted him that which he requested."1 Chronicles 4:10, KJV

Jabez had a different kind of altar standing over him: a name. "His mother called his name Jabez, saying, Because I bare him with sorrow" (1 Chronicles 4:9). Every time someone called him, they were saying "sorrow." His mother did not mean him harm. She was naming her pain. But the label stuck to her son. Jabez did not fight his mother. He did not curse his village. He prayed. And "God granted him that which he requested."

So what is the altar in your father's house? Sometimes it is literal: a shrine, a dedication, charms you were given as a child, marks made on your body, an oath taken on your behalf. Sometimes it is a name or a label. Sometimes it is a practice you still take part in every time you go home. Whatever it is, the way forward is the same. Renounce it before God. Get rid of anything connected to it, as the believers at Ephesus burned their books of magic in public (Acts 19:18-19). And then do the second half of Gideon's instruction, the part people forget: "build an altar unto the LORD thy God."

You do not just tear down. You build. An altar of prayer in your home. An altar of the Word in your morning. An altar of worship in your family. The Bible warns that "the curse causeless shall not come" (Proverbs 26:2). Close every door you can find, and fill your house with something stronger than what used to stand there.

And hear this clearly: throwing down the altar is not the same as attacking your family. Gideon broke an idol, not his father's bones. Fight the altar. Love the people.

Pray this

  • Every altar in my father's house and my mother's house speaking against my life, be thrown down by the power of God (Judges 6:25).
  • I renounce every dedication, oath and covenant made for me without the Lord. I belong to Jesus.
  • Every name and label of sorrow over my life, Lord, change it as You did for Jabez (1 Chronicles 4:10).
  • I build an altar to the Lord in my home. Let His fire never go out there (Leviticus 6:13).

Chapter Six

He Led Him Out of the Village

"And he cometh to Bethsaida; and they bring a blind man unto him, and besought him to touch him. And he took the blind man by the hand, and led him out of the town... And he sent him away to his house, saying, Neither go into the town, nor tell it to any in the town."
Mark 8:22-23, 26 — KJV

Jesus could have healed this man anywhere. He healed people in streets, in houses, in synagogues, even from miles away with a word. But in Bethsaida, He did something unusual. He "took the blind man by the hand, and led him out of the town." The word Mark uses for "town" is the same Greek word for "village" he uses for the place where the colt was tied. Before Jesus opened this man's eyes, He walked him out of the village.

Why Bethsaida? Because Jesus pronounced woe on it: "Woe unto thee, Bethsaida! for if the mighty works, which were done in you, had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago" (Matthew 11:21). Bethsaida had seen miracle after miracle and stayed exactly the same. It was a place where unbelief had become the atmosphere. Some environments are so thick with doubt, fear and old agreements that Jesus will not perform your miracle there. He will take you by the hand and walk you out first.

Do not miss the tenderness. "He took the blind man by the hand." This man could not see the road. He could not find the way out on his own. Jesus did not shout directions at him from a distance. He held his hand and walked with him, step by step, out of the place that had kept him blind. If you cannot see your way out, you do not need to. You only need to let Him hold your hand.

"I see men as trees, walking."Mark 8:24, KJV

Then comes the only healing in the Gospels that happens in two stages. Jesus spat on the man's eyes, laid hands on him, and "asked him if he saw ought." The man looked up and said, "I see men as trees, walking." Something had changed, but it was not finished. So Jesus "put his hands again upon his eyes, and made him look up: and he was restored, and saw every man clearly" (v.25).

That is a word for some of you. You have prayed. Something shifted. You can see better than before, but things are still blurry. You are tempted to think the deliverance failed. It did not. Jesus is not finished. He touched this man twice, and He will touch you again. Do not stop at "men as trees." Keep looking up until you see every man clearly.

And then the instruction that matters most: "Neither go into the town." Jesus sent him home, but not back into Bethsaida. Freedom does not always mean cutting off your people. It does mean refusing to walk back into the atmosphere that blinded you. For some, that means not returning to the shrine at the family ceremony. For some, it means not taking part in the practices you have renounced. For some, it means not going back into the conversations, the fear and the agreements that kept you bound.

Jesus led you out. Do not walk yourself back in.

Pray this

  • Lord Jesus, take me by the hand and lead me out of every village that has kept me blind (Mark 8:23).
  • Touch my eyes again. I will not settle for "men as trees." Let me see clearly (Mark 8:25).
  • I refuse to walk back into the atmosphere You brought me out of.
  • Every spirit of unbelief around my life, I renounce you. I choose faith.

Chapter Seven

Stopped at the Gate

"Now when he came nigh to the gate of the city, behold, there was a dead man carried out, the only son of his mother, and she was a widow: and much people of the city was with her. And when the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her, and said unto her, Weep not. And he came and touched the bier: and they that bare him stood still. And he said, Young man, I say unto thee, Arise."
Luke 7:12-14 — KJV

Two crowds met at the gate of Nain. One was coming in, following Jesus: His disciples and "much people" (Luke 7:11). The other was going out, following a coffin: a widow, her only son on an open bier, and "much people of the city." Life was walking in. Death was walking out. And they met at the gate.

Everything about that funeral procession had been decided by the village. The young man was dead. The mourners were hired. The grave was dug. The whole town had agreed on how this story ended and was walking it out together, step by step, to the burial ground. Nobody in that crowd was praying for a resurrection. They were carrying out a verdict.

Some of you have watched your village carry out the same verdict on person after person. The same early deaths. The same ruined marriages. The same brilliant young people who go abroad and come home with nothing. Everybody walks in the procession. Everybody says, "It is our lot." And quietly, you have started to feel the bier under your own back.

Then Jesus. Luke says, "when the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her." Before He did anything powerful, He felt something. He saw a widow who had already buried her husband and was now burying her only son, her last support, her future. And His first words were not a sermon. They were, "Weep not."

"And he came and touched the bier: and they that bare him stood still."Luke 7:14, KJV

Then He did the unthinkable. He walked up to the procession and touched the bier. In that culture, touching anything connected to the dead made you unclean. Jesus did not care. He laid His hand on the very thing that was carrying the young man to the grave. "And they that bare him stood still."

Stop there. The bearers stopped. The procession stopped. Everything that had been carrying this young man toward burial froze at the touch of Jesus. That is what happens when He lays His hand on your situation. The forces that were carrying your life toward a grave, the patterns, the agreements, the spiritual bearers you could not see, all of them stand still.

And then He spoke to the young man, not to the bearers, not to the crowd: "Young man, I say unto thee, Arise." Elijah had stretched himself on a dead boy three times and prayed (1 Kings 17:21). Elisha, in nearby Shunem, had lain on a dead child and walked the house before he came back to life (2 Kings 4:34-35). Jesus simply said, "I say unto thee." No ritual. No struggle. His own word, on His own authority. "And he that was dead sat up, and began to speak. And he delivered him to his mother" (v.15).

He gave him back. Not to the grave, not to the village verdict, but to his family, alive and speaking. The same Jesus is standing at the gate of your situation today. Whatever your village has decided is final, it is not final until He says so. And He is saying, "Arise."

Pray this

  • Lord Jesus, touch the bier of every situation that is being carried to burial in my life (Luke 7:14).
  • Every power carrying my future toward death and loss, stand still at the gate, in Jesus' name.
  • I hear the voice of the Son of God: I arise from every place of death, loss and stagnation.
  • Lord, give back to my family everything the village has carried out. Restore it alive.

Chapter Eight

Fruitful in the Land of My Affliction

"And Joseph called the name of the firstborn Manasseh: For God, said he, hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house. And the name of the second called he Ephraim: For God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction."
Genesis 41:51-52 — KJV

Nobody in Scripture had more reason to hate his family than Joseph. His brothers threw him in a pit, sold him to traders and told their father he was dead. He spent years as a slave in a foreign land and more years in a prison for a crime he did not commit. If anyone had the right to blame his village for his struggle, it was Joseph.

But look at what he named his sons. The first: Manasseh, "God hath made me forget all my toil, and all my father's house." Joseph was not saying he lost his memory. He was saying the pain of his father's house no longer ruled him. The second: Ephraim, "God hath caused me to be fruitful in the land of my affliction." Not after he got home. Not once the brothers apologized. In the land of affliction itself, far from home, God made him fruitful.

That is God's word to everyone who left home and has been struggling ever since. The place of your affliction can become the place of your fruitfulness. Through Jeremiah, God told His exiled people in Babylon: "seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it: for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace" (Jeremiah 29:7). Build houses. Plant gardens. Prosper where you are. Your breakthrough is not waiting for you to move back. It is waiting for you to be free where you are.

"But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive."Genesis 50:20, KJV

And then Joseph's story does something this book must not leave out. When famine came, the brothers who sold him came to Egypt to buy bread. Joseph had the power to destroy them. Instead he wept, forgave them, and fed the whole family. "Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good." The man who had been detained by his family became the man who delivered his family.

That is the end of freedom. Not revenge. Not running from your people forever. Remember the man Jesus set free in the country of the Gadarenes. He begged to go with Jesus, and Jesus said no: "Go home to thy friends, and tell them how great things the Lord hath done for thee, and hath had compassion on thee" (Mark 5:19). Some of you were set free so that one day you could go back to the village with a different power than the one that held you. Nehemiah prospered in the king's palace in Susa, then wept for his broken home city and went back to rebuild its walls (Nehemiah 1-2).

Remember the brother from New York at the beginning of this book. He did not just get his freedom. He became a pastor. The captive became the one who proclaims liberty. That is the pattern of the kingdom.

So here is my charge to you as this book closes. Leave the village's chains, not the village's people. Be fruitful in the land where you are. Forgive the ones who thought evil against you. And when God has made you whole, go home, and tell them how great things the Lord has done.

Pray this

  • Lord, make me forget the toil and the pain of my father's house. Let it rule me no more (Genesis 41:51).
  • Make me fruitful in the land of my affliction. I receive power to get wealth where You have planted me (Deuteronomy 8:18).
  • I forgive those who thought evil against me. Turn it for good, to save much people alive (Genesis 50:20).
  • Lord, make me a carrier of blessing back to my village, not bondage.

The Freedom Section

Seven Steps, One Prayer, Your Answer

"Jesus saith unto them, Loose him, and let him go."
John 11:44 — KJV

When Jesus raised Lazarus, Lazarus came out of the tomb alive but still wrapped in grave clothes. Jesus did not unwrap him personally. He turned to the people around him and said, "Loose him, and let him go." Life comes from Jesus. But walking out of the wrappings is something you do, with help. Here are seven steps. Take them slowly, and come back to them as often as you need.

Seven steps to freedom

  1. Come to Jesus for yourself. "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed" (John 8:36). Freedom starts with a covenant change, not a ritual. If you have never given your life to Jesus Christ, do it now: confess Him as Lord and believe God raised Him from the dead (Romans 10:9).
  2. Repent and renounce. "Cast away from you all your transgressions" (Ezekiel 18:31). "We have renounced the hidden things" (2 Corinthians 4:2). Name before God anything you took part in: shrines, charms, oaths, dedications, initiations. Repent of your own sins too, not only your family's.
  3. Throw down the altar. "Throw down the altar of Baal that thy father hath" (Judges 6:25). Get rid of charms and occult objects, as the believers at Ephesus burned their books (Acts 19:18-19). Stop taking part in practices you have renounced.
  4. Forgive your people. "When ye stand praying, forgive, if ye have ought against any" (Mark 11:25). This is the same chapter as the tied colt. Release your relatives to God by name, in private. Forgiveness is not approval. It is you refusing to stay tied to them through bitterness.
  5. Pray with authority. "I will contend with him that contendeth with thee" (Isaiah 49:25). Pray the prophetic prayer below out loud. If the burden is heavy, pray it with your pastor or a mature believer (James 5:16). Fasting can help you focus (Matthew 17:21).
  6. Close the doors. "Neither give place to the devil" (Ephesians 4:27). "The curse causeless shall not come" (Proverbs 26:2). Deal with habits, fear, debt patterns, ungodly vows, and the words you speak over yourself (Proverbs 18:21).
  7. Walk it out and build. "It is he that giveth thee power to get wealth" (Deuteronomy 8:18). Make a plan. Grow a skill. Save. Set loving limits on demands from home (1 Timothy 5:8). Stay planted in a church family. And tell your testimony (Mark 5:19).

A word of care: if you are in deep distress, have frightening experiences, or find you cannot function, pray, and also reach out to your pastor and to a doctor. Not every struggle is a spirit, and getting help is not a lack of faith. And never accuse a relative, especially an elderly person or a child, of witchcraft. Fight the altar. Love the people.

The prophetic deliverance prayer

Find a quiet place. Stand if you can. Pray this out loud, with faith, and do not rush.

I come out. Father, I come to You in the name of Jesus Christ, who was made a curse for me. I confess Him as my Lord and my Redeemer. I repent of every sin I have committed, and I renounce every altar, covenant, oath, dedication and agreement made by me or for me in my father's house or my mother's house. I was not redeemed with silver and gold from the empty ways handed down from my fathers. I was redeemed with the precious blood of Jesus. That blood speaks for me today.

Loose him. Every power sitting in the lurking places of the village, watching my life in secret, hear the word of the Lord: the Lord hath need of me. In the name of Jesus, I loose my destiny from every post it was tied to. Every net spread for my feet, break. Every handwriting of ordinances written against me, be blotted out by the cross. Every procession carrying my future to burial, stand still at the gate. I am not the lawful captive of any village, any spirit, or any family pattern. The Lord contends with them that contend with me.

Arise. I hear Jesus say, "Arise." I arise from stagnation, from repeated loss, from the ceiling my family could not break. The snare is broken, and I am escaped. My body left the village, and today my life leaves it too. I will be fruitful in the land of my affliction. I forgive my people, and I will carry blessing back to my village, not bondage. Whom the Son sets free is free indeed. In Jesus' mighty name, amen.
