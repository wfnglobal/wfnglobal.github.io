Nothing New Under the Sun · Volume One · Still Speaking

The Cave

Chapter 1 · mirrors 1 Samuel 24

The chapter as a film, with an original piano score · 8 minutes

Swipe or scroll sideways to turn the pages · 15 pages

Maya had eleven minutes to answer an email that could decide the next ten years of her life.

It arrived at 8:49 on a Tuesday, which is the most dangerous time of the week. Monday is for surviving. By Wednesday everybody is tired. Tuesday is when people get brave.

The email was from Tessa in Finance. Tessa did not send emails. Tessa sent sticky notes, and sometimes a look. So when the subject line said only "Read this before 9," Maya closed her office door, which she never did, and read it.

Attached was a spreadsheet. Seven rows, highlighted yellow. Each one was an expense claim, and each one had been approved by the same person who had submitted it. Hotel rooms on nights with no meetings. A "client dinner" for four on a Sunday, at a restaurant forty minutes from any client. Thirty-one thousand dollars, give or take, all signed off by Graham Pierce, Vice President of Operations.

Graham Pierce was Maya's boss.

Graham Pierce was also, as of nine o'clock this morning, going to stand in front of the board and present a restructuring plan. Maya had seen a draft. On page six, in a box with a soft grey border, as if a soft border made it kinder, her role had been "consolidated." That is the word companies use when they mean "removed," but want it to sound like housekeeping.

Under the spreadsheet, Tessa had written three lines.

"The board gets this, he's gone by lunch. Nobody would blame you. You have until 9."

Here is something you should know about Maya Brooks. She was good at her job, which was the problem. Two years ago she had fixed a shipping contract nobody else could fix, and the CEO had mentioned her by name in the all-staff meeting. Graham had smiled, and clapped, and from that day on he had not stopped trying to make her small. Meetings she was not invited to. Credit that went to other people. A performance review that used the word "abrasive" three times, and her name once.

She had never said a word against him. Not in the kitchen, not on the group chat, not to her mother, who would have said several words and meant all of them.

And now the whole thing was sitting in her inbox, yellow and quiet, waiting.

Maya did what she always did when she could not think. She took her phone and walked to the stairwell.

The east stairwell of the Calder building is not a pretty place. The paint is the colour of old porridge. One light has been flickering since spring. But nobody uses it, because there are lifts, and so it is the only room on the fourteenth floor where nobody is watching you. She went down half a flight and sat on the steps under the red EXIT sign, in the dark, and read Tessa's email again.

8:52

Then the door above her opened.

She knew the shoes before she saw the man. Brown, polished to a shine, and loud on concrete. Graham Pierce came into the stairwell, let the door close behind him, and leaned against the wall on the landing. He did not look down. People rarely look down.

He was on the phone.

"No, I know. I know." His voice was not his meeting voice. It was smaller. "Just get her to the appointment, and I'll come straight after. Tell her I'm coming. Tell her..." He stopped. He pressed his free hand flat against his forehead, the way you do when a headache is really something else. "Tell her Dad's coming."

Maya did not breathe.

He was six steps above her. Alone. No board, no slides, no soft grey borders. Just a tired man in good shoes, in the dark, who had no idea that the person he was about to remove from the company was sitting close enough to hear his heart.

Her phone buzzed. Tessa.

"Have you sent it?? 7 mins."

This is the part of the story where most of us would like to believe we know what we would do. We don't. Nobody does until the stairwell.

Maya opened the spreadsheet. She did not forward it. She did something smaller, the kind of thing you do to tell yourself you are not really doing anything. She took a screenshot, and she saved it to a folder on her phone called "Just in case."

It took two seconds. It felt like nothing.

Then it felt like something.

It sat in her chest like a stone she had swallowed. She looked up at the man on the landing, who was saying, "Okay. Okay. Love you too," into his phone, and she felt the strangest thing. She did not feel safe. She did not feel clever. She felt ashamed, as if she had reached up and cut a piece off his jacket while he was not looking.

Graham put the phone in his pocket. He stood there for three more seconds, the longest three seconds of the morning. Then he fixed his tie, opened the door, and went back up into the light. He never knew she was there.

8:56

Maya looked at Tessa's message for a long time. Then she typed:

"No. Not like this. If this has to come out, it won't come out through me."

She pressed send before she could think of a better reason not to.

Tessa wrote back one word: "Why?"

And Maya, who had an answer for most things, found that she did not have a good one. Not one that would fit in a text message, anyway. She only knew that some doors, once you push them, do not close again. And she knew, the way you know your own handwriting, that if she won this way, she would have to live with the person who won.

8:59

She stood up, brushed off the back of her skirt, and walked upstairs to the meeting where her job was about to disappear.

The screenshot was still in her phone. She had not deleted it. She was not that holy yet.

But she was learning.

It has happened before

The cave at En-gedi

And he came to the sheepcotes by the way, where was a cave; and Saul went in to cover his feet: and David and his men remained in the sides of the cave. And the men of David said unto him, Behold the day of which the LORD said unto thee, Behold, I will deliver thine enemy into thine hand, that thou mayest do to him as it shall seem good unto thee. Then David arose, and cut off the skirt of Saul's robe privily. And it came to pass afterward, that David's heart smote him, because he had cut off Saul's skirt. And he said unto his men, The LORD forbid that I should do this thing unto my master, the LORD's anointed, to stretch forth mine hand against him, seeing he is the anointed of the LORD. So David stayed his servants with these words, and suffered them not to rise against Saul. But Saul rose up out of the cave, and went on his way.

1 Samuel 24:3-7 · KJV

The LORD judge between me and thee, and the LORD avenge me of thee: but mine hand shall not be upon thee.

1 Samuel 24:12 · KJV

Three thousand years ago, a king named Saul spent years hunting a young man named David. David's crime was being good at his job. The women of Israel sang that Saul had killed his thousands and David his ten thousands, and Saul never forgave the song.

One day Saul walked into a cave near En-gedi to relieve himself. Alone. Unguarded. He had no idea David and his men were already inside, in the dark. David's men said what Tessa said: this is your day, God has handed him to you. David crept close enough to touch him, and cut off a corner of his robe. That was all. And then the Bible says something remarkable: David's heart smote him. He felt guilty for the corner.

Notice what David did not say. He did not say Saul was a good king. He did not say Saul deserved mercy. He said, in effect: this is not my hand's job. The LORD judge between me and thee.

Some of us are standing in a stairwell right now. The proof is in our phone. The person who hurt us is six steps away, and everybody around us is saying, "Send it." The question in this chapter is not whether they deserve it. It is whose hand gets to do it.
